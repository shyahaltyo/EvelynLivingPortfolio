What was the "SLAP! - a field trip wristband"?

At this point, I've completely two projects with mostly the same teammates, and I was no where near comfortable working with a new team. So when Praxis II came along and assigned me into a group with new faces, I felt like I was back in the first month of Praxis I again.

But this time was different - I had the knowledge and experience from before. Seeing how the RFP, Beta Release, and Showcase were just upgraded, more in-depth versions of the original assignments in Praxis I, it gave me the confidence to get comfortable with my new team. This time, we had the opportunity to put our engineering design skills in use for a real community: the Balmy Beach Daycare Centre, whose goal was to increase the time efficiency of applying wristbands onto children.

Framing the project: The Praxis Definition of a Community

In Praxis II, we learned that a community consists of one or more individuals that are united by location, identity, organization, or interest. Our project required us to collaborate with one of these communities in the GTA, but before we could reach out, we had to do our "Due Diligence": We had to understand and approach a community in their perspective to avoid causing harm to members or treating people like things.

How to engage with a community

To demonstrate due diligence, we conducted secondary research to better engage with the community. This included recognizing the activities, goals, and risks in the community, and doing so also helped us prepare thoughtful questions for the community when we do establish contact.

We eventually had the opportunity to connect with Site 3 CoLaboratory. As a team, we decided to take the next steps to properly engage with the community and represent our team's wish to collaborate with them. They validated our intentions, and also verified that there were indeed aspects in the community that we could lend a hand in: preventing airborne micro-fibres, which are released by tufting in the crafting area, from entering non-tufting areas.

Confirming this problem with the community gave us the green light for us to frame an opportunity for this issue. Through continuous communication with the community, we could verify the stakeholder needs and frame a design space for the opportunity that validated these needs. Eventually, we developed a request for proposal on preventing airborne tufting fibres in Site 3.

Diverging: Acknowledging Team Values

Unfortunately, due to Praxis II restrictions, we weren't able to continue collaborating with Site 3 and instead had to pivot to another opportunity: increasing the time efficiency of applying wristbands at the Balmy Beach Daycare. In the new RFP, they framed a design space that suggested a solution through mechanical means, like a machine that applied wristbands. With this new opportunity, we had to again work as a team to devise a solution that can be validated and accepted by the community.

Using Morph Charts to incorportae our values

As a team, we didn't quite agree with the provided design space because the solutions in the space didn't seem to address the root cause of why the process was inefficent. The director of Balmy Beach Centre, Christine, indicated that the most time-consuming step of applying wristbands was adjusting the size of the wristband for each child, cutting off the excess, and having to cut of the entire bracelet if it was applied incorrectly.

Additionally, our team values empathy and systemic engineering, and we hoped that every stakeholder, from staff, students, to parents, were able to participate in the process of wearing wristbands. If we simply made a machine to apply wristbands to children, it felt like we were thinking of the children as "things that we needed to get wristbands on perfectly". We wondered if we could give the children a bigger role in wearing wristbands...such as making them wear it themselves!

We diverged into the initial SLAP! design, which featured a slapband and with a flaps with locking buttons. We sent several emails to the community confirming the cognitive abilities of children, and evaluated that they were able to slap the wrist onto their wrist with 2 simple steps. We also considered the parents' expectations, which see the staff as the authority in the process. Therefore, the locking flap was added as a "verification cue,": the staff had to lock the buttons using the flaps after the child has worn their slapband to both confirm that it was worn correctly and prevent it from being taken off during field trips.

Represent: Verifying and Validating

Although we now had a design that seemed like a solution, we had to actually verify that the features worked, and acquire the community's validation for the idea. In engineering design, these confirming these aspects are important to ensure that a bridges don't fall after being built.

Letting the audience believe in a design

Verification shows that our design actually meets the needs from the community and our own requirements. Testing for evaluation criteria can show that the new design performs better than the original, or demonstrating that the improved requirements are met.

Other times, we researched materials that would work well on our design, such as PVC or fluoroelastomer, since we lacked the resources to acquire these supplies. Then, we conducted to design the dimensions of the wristband using the researched material properties such that it would meet the requirement metrics.

We kept the concerns of the community in mind, even if it wasn't necessarily a Praxis requirement, such as cost. Our wristband was new from the original, so we designed it to be reusable to accomodate for the new product. The cost efficiency, along with our other design decisions, helped us gain validation for our SLAP! from the community。

Wrapping up the Wristbands

Working with the communities really gave me a mental "checklist" when designing for a solution. It was up to our team to prove to stakeholders, such as the community members and the teaching team, that our recommended design is the best solution to the opportunity. Just like developing a design, convincing the audience to believe in our design is also an iterative process.

Now that we have gone through multiple rounds of verifying and validating our ideas, the next step in engineering design would be launching the idea in production...
