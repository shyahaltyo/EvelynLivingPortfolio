What was the "Sussy Konstruksyon Kwaltie" Bridge?

In the same term as ESC101, I also had a structures and materials course, CIV102, where we learned all about bridges, Newton's Third Law, and concrete. At the end of this course, there was another team project, and I was allowed to pick my own team this time. Seeing how I effectively collaborated with my Praxis I team, 3 of us decided to get back into a team together, along with another member that we've previously worked together in tutorials and labs.

Our team had the opportunity to make our own beam bridge out of matboard, a dense cardboard material. These bridges would then be tested for their strength (maximum load) and material efficiency (ratio between maximum load and weight). They were placed across a length of roughly 1250mm, and a model 3-cart train would run over the bridge deck. The mass of the train would be incrementally increased until the bridge fell.

Framing the project: Delegating Tasks for Teamwork

Since everyone in our team had worked with one another before, we all had a sense of each others' strengths and abilities. Towards the first Deliverable, which was composed of force calculations for a initial given design, we planned out the tasks that each member should focus on. For me, I felt more confident with the mechanical end, such as designing the bridge dimensions and prototyping.

Group work is built off of independent work

From Praxis I, we learned about the importance of having time to work together as a team. Group working sessions allowed us to complete tasks in parallel, so that we could build off of each others efforts to make a collaborative result. Activities such as revising designs, writing long and complicated code, or even gathering materials, should be done as a team.

However, many of these tasks are also relies on the fact that each team member has completed their part. Otherwise, a group working session might just consist of each member working on their delegated tasks in the same room, which doesn't allow members to reinforce each others' idea or effort. Without team feedback, we wouldn't be able to collectively recommend an idea, since each member has kept their perspective to themselves, and that would not be good engineering design if a team cannot agree on what idea to represent.

In our team, our group working sessions were generally productive, because each member in our team had faith that the other members would attend the meeting with their part done well. With this trust, we could work in parallel every week or two, framing our project by continuously delegating new tasks, planning next steps, and revising design decisions.

Converging: Constrained by Resources

The bridge project was unique compared to Praxis because we had limited resources: the matboard was only 1015x810mm big, we had 2 tubes of contact cement, and we had a deadline to complete the bridge, which meant extra time needed to be allocated to allow the cement to dry. Additionally, we didn't have nearly as much freedom to reframe our opportunity in methods such as altering the bridge type.

Managing risk to create the ideal design

Despite all these restrictions in dimension and material, we still had the choice to decide what we wanted to focus on for the bridge. We could aim for the strongest bridge, where we use all our available materials to try to maximize the weight that our bridge could carry, or we could try to optimize material and strength by designing a bridge with high strength-to-weight ratio.

Since we decided to focus on pure strength, we designed our bridge dimension with reference to the matboard. We realized that altering different parts of the bridge could yield significant or little changes with high or low risk. For example, adding extra diaphragms would greatly increase the bridge's plate buckling ratio, while being low risk becausee the diaphragms did not take much material and fit into the design nicely.

The decision to use the entire matboard was identified by us to be high risk. Every part of the matboard belonged to an important piece on the bridge, so to avoid cutting or accuracy errors, we ensured each piece was slightly larger than the required calculation. The entire process of building the bridge demonstrated how it's in the hands of the team to represent these uncertainties in engineering design.

Represent: Bringing the Design to Reality

Before we even built the bridge, we had taken steps to demonstrate that our bridge could really meet the goal of maximizing strength. We only had one attempt to create the bridge due to the limited materials and time, so we had to be confident that our design would perform well.

Prototyping the unbelievable

By using prototypes, we could simulate running the train test over the bridge, demonstrating that our design could work even if it didn't exist yet. As a team, we each worked on different prototype representations, working with our strengths as a joint effort to make our bridge come to life.

Our prototypes included building a mini-version of the bridge, writing code to calculate the strength of the bridge, and designing a 3D CAD model to show dimensions of the bridge. These prototypes reflected our idea of the bridge at that point in time, and were revised throughout the project to match new design decisions.

Yet these prototypes were not an accurate representation of what happened in testing. The mathematical prototype that we programmed predicted a maximum load of 883N, and that failure would occur by compression at the centre of the bridge. However, testing revealed that our bridge actually failed at 133 N.

Lessons from a Fallen Bridge

Although the "Sussy Konstruksyon Kwaltie" Bridge eventually did fall, it developed my understanding of engineering design. I experienced the process of bringing an idea to reality as a team after deciding on a recommended design, which came with a lot of planning and unseen obstacles. In our case, we have the chance to fail and retry without too much consequences (mostly just material costs and ego), but that might not be the case in the future, emphasizing the stakes engineers hold when they recommend a design.

Therefore, the next step in engineering design should be conducting verification and validation tests, ensuring that reality can reflect the model accurately...
