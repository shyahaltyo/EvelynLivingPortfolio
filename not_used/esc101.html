What was the "Umbrella Mop Spinner"?

Praxis I was my first experience working in a university group project. I was assigned to a team of people that I've only known for less than a month. Yet from the moment we spoke to each other during Studio, we immediately clicked: we gave off the same energy, we were all excited to be in EngSci, and we were all commuters that were tired of carrying wet umbrellas on campus.

So when it was time for us to pick out a "spartz" - an annoying inconvenience - we took this opportunity to find a method to prevent wet umbrellas from dripping onto the floor. Thus, this "spartz" was the topic in focus for our Design Brief, Alpha Release, and Design Report.

Framing the project: Designing the Design Space

In our project, we had the freedom to design our own design space. Us, being the team working on this project, were crucial stakeholders for this opportunity. We had firsthand experience of the consequences from dripping umbrellas on campus floors, and we had the capability to take action and improve the situation. Therefore, we could greatly influence the opportunity's framing.

Where PIAA makes its entrance

However, we were not the sole stakeholders. Had we acted immediately and tried to fix the problem rashly, we would've neglected many other stakeholders for this opportunity, such as other students, staff or caretaking on UofT campus. This would go against my core idea of engineering design, which is openly exploring different perspectives. Additionally, trying to act without properly framing could suggest that anything is a solution, so there wouldn't be much of an opportunity in the first place.

We began by listing out all the possible stakeholders that could influence our opportunity. Then, we tried to look at the situation from their perspective, inspecting what needs, goals, and objectives they might require for a solution. After that, we assessed how much they would actually affect our opportunity by organizing the stakeholders into primary, secondary, and tertiary ranks.

By perceiving, inspecting, and assessing the the stakeholders before acting towards a solution, we were able to achieve a design space that wasn't too loose on topics like safety, convenience, or functionality. It was also not too restrictive, so we actually had a chance to brainstorm possible solutions. We would continuoutly revise these rankings and stakeholders throughout the project whenever the space felt too restricted or too loose.

Diverging: Brainstorming Ideas

After we had established a design space, we began to brainstorm how a solution might look like. We had many ideas that popped up intuitively, such as using bags or blow drying umbrellas. Yet, randomly picking an idea as the "solution" would've contradicted my belief in engineering design, which promotes iterative processes for finding solutions! Therefore, it was necessary to look outwards and diverge a wide variety of ideas before giving the verdict.

References inspires Innovation

The first step we took in our diverging process was freewriting random ideas, but after a while we would run out of creativity and write down mostly ridiculous ideas for fun. Nevertheless, we were still able to get decent list, and so we decided to diverge using another strategy.

To achieve this, we conducted secondary research on reference designs that had similar mechanisms for removing water off of surfaces. Some of these were not meant to be used on umbrellas, so we looked deeper into how we could modify them to work on umbrellas. They had to meet various goals, such as a long enough force, taking up appropiate amounts of space, and being convenient enough such that people would prefer it over just carrying a wet umbrella.

We could then conduct primary research on these ideas using our own observations and testing to further refine the designs. Sometimes, this led to us brainstorming to diverge and improve the current designs.

Converging: Choosing the Best Recommendation

From these observations and tests, we collected data on our designs and evaluated how each design performed in each requirement using a combined measurement matric and pugh chart. There were multiple rounds of these charts, which helped us understand what part of a design made it perform better in a requirement compared to another design.

Comparison is not always a Competition

These charts were evaluated heuristically, which meant we didn't completely eliminate a design when it seemed like it was a "worse" design from the others. Since we all of the requirements were not of equal importance, a design that seemed to excel in more requirements was not necessarily better than a design that excelled in less.

Instead, we used these measurement matrices and pugh charts to decide how we could combine, modify, or eliminate (SCAMPER) specific features of a design to improve how it performed on certain criteria. If only a single design excelled when evaluated, we would analyze what made it perform well and try to apply it to other designs.

Eventually, through the use of other converging techniques like pairwise comparisons and prototyping, we decided to recommend the umbrella mop spinner, which upon inserting an umbrella into the mechanism, would use centrifugal force to spin off water on the surface of an umbrella.

Closing the Umbrella

From framing with PIAA, to diverging with research, to converging with charts, I began to establish the effort needed to responsibly recommend a design. In other words, I learned about the process it takes to continuously reiterate an idea by thinking about why we chose to include specific features, and how they act as a solution for the opportunity. This required being open to completely changing our original idea, which may have been more intuitive (bags to cover the wet umbrella), and exploring the creativity of a spinner for umbrellas through engineering design.

Then, the next step in engineering design after establishing the recommended idea should be attempting to bring it to reality...
